What is Accountant Mode

Accountant Mode turns your Daily Cash Register entries into ready-to-use accounting reports: P&L (Profit and Loss Statement), Financial Score and MEI tax estimate. You start speaking the same language as your accountant, with reliable, organized numbers.

Instead of finding out at the end of the month whether the store made a profit or a loss, you track in real time how every dollar that comes in and goes out impacts your business result.

Who it is for: any retailer who wants to truly understand how much the store makes — whether it is a bakery, thrift store, clothing boutique, pet shop, or any other small business — without building spreadsheets or paying for expensive software.
Important: Accountant Mode works based on the entries you make in the Daily Cash Register. If you do not log a sale, it will not appear in the P&L. Accurate entries = accurate reports.

How the Automatic P&L Works

The P&L (Profit and Loss statement) is the report that shows whether your store made money or not over a given period. Accountant Mode builds this report automatically from the categories you select when recording each transaction in the cash register.

Transactions are sorted into four groups. Here is how each one works in practice:

Group 1

Revenue (everything tagged as Sale)

Any entry categorized as Sale contributes to the Gross Revenue line of the P&L. It is the top of the statement: how much the store billed before any discount or cost.

Example 1 — Bakery: Day's sales: 12 cakes ($80 each) + 30 chocolate truffles ($5 each) = $1,110 logged as Sale. That is the figure that goes into the Revenue line of the P&L.
Example 2 — Thrift store: Sold 5 pieces on Saturday for $250 total, logged as Sale. The following week, another $180. The P&L accumulates $430 in Revenue for the period.
Example 3 — Accessories boutique: In-store sale: $120. Instagram order paid via bank transfer: $340. Both logged as Sale. The system totals both: $460 in Revenue.
Group 2

COGS — Cost of Goods Sold (Replenishment and raw materials)

Everything you bought to resell or use in production goes here. It is the direct cost of what you sold. The difference between Revenue and COGS is your Gross Profit.

Example 1 — Bakery: Flour, eggs, butter and packaging boxes purchased for the week's production: $95. Logged as Replenishment/Raw Material. This amount goes into COGS.
Example 2 — Clothing store: 20 t-shirts bought wholesale for $150 to resell. Logged as Replenishment. The system deducts from Gross Profit: Revenue $300 - COGS $150 = Gross Profit $150.
Example 3 — Pet shop: Dog food, cat litter and grooming products purchased for resale: $230. These are direct inputs for what will be sold. They go into COGS and reduce gross profit for the month.

Group 3

Operating Expenses (Packaging, Shipping, Other)

These are costs not directly tied to the product itself but necessary to run the store. They include everything you spend to keep the business operating day to day.

Example 1 — Cosmetics store: Branded bags, tape and tissue paper for gift wrapping: $35. Logged as Packaging. Goes into Operating Expenses.
Example 2 — Online store: Postal shipping costs for the week's orders: $52. Payment processor fee: $14. Both logged as Shipping and Other, respectively. They reduce EBITDA.
Example 3 — Bakery: Gas for the oven ($32), prorated electricity for the month ($22), cleaning supplies ($11). All logged under Other/Operating. Accountant Mode totals them automatically in this line.
Example 4 — Clothing store: Studio rent: $200, internet: $25, management software: $12. Fixed monthly operating expenses. They all go into this group and the P&L reflects them every month.
Group 4

Owner Pay (withdrawals flagged as Personal)

This is your own salary as the business owner. When you take money from the register for personal expenses, it must be logged with the Personal flag. The system separates it from operations and displays it in the P&L owner pay line.

Example 1 — Bakery: Every Friday the owner withdraws $125 for household expenses. Logged as Personal. At month's end, the P&L shows $500 in owner pay, cleanly separated from business costs.
Example 2 — Thrift store: Personal credit card payment made with cash from the register: $87. Logged as Personal. Accountant Mode does not confuse this with an operating expense — it goes into the owner pay group.
Example 3 — Accessories boutique: Two partners each withdraw $200 on the 15th and on the 30th. All four withdrawals logged as Personal. The P&L shows total owner pay of $800 for the month.

Financial Score: the 4 pillars of store health

The Score (0 to 100) evaluates your store's financial health across four dimensions. It does not measure profit alone — it measures the overall solidity of the business. A store with a high score is predictable, sustainable and ready to grow.

Pillar 1

Margin — real profit as a percentage of revenue

Measures net profit as a share of revenue. Businesses with margins below 10% are in the risk zone: any unexpected expense turns into a loss.

High score: Bakery with Revenue $1,500 and Net Profit $375 = Margin 25%. High score on this pillar.
Low score: Clothing store with Revenue $2,500 and Net Profit $100 = Margin 4%. The score flags that the operation is consuming almost all the revenue.
How to improve: Review the selling price of your best-moving products. A small price adjustment (+$1 or +$2) often raises the margin significantly without pushing customers away.
Pillar 2

Controlled owner pay — personal withdrawals under 30% of revenue

Withdrawing more than the business can sustain is one of the main reasons small businesses fail. The system automatically checks whether your owner pay is within a healthy limit.

High score: Pet shop with Revenue $1,250 and owner pay of $300 (24% of revenue). Within the limit. High score.
Low score: Thrift store with Revenue $800 and owner pay of $400 (50% of revenue). The owner is withdrawing more than the business can support. Low score and risk alert.
How to improve: Set a fixed monthly owner pay — treat it like a salary. Do not withdraw based on what you need at the moment; that destabilizes cash flow and the P&L.
Pillar 3

Diversification — not relying on a single product or customer

If 70% of your revenue comes from one product or one customer, any change to that source can bring the entire income down. The score penalizes this dependency.

High score: Cosmetics store selling moisturizers, perfumes, makeup and hair care. No single product accounts for more than 30% of revenue. Diversified and resilient.
Low score: Bakery where 80% of revenue comes from one custom cake. If demand for that product drops, the store goes with it.
How to improve: Introduce complementary products at a lower price point to spread revenue across more items. Bundles and kits help distribute income without large investment.
Pillar 4

Consistency — steady monthly revenue over time

A store that brings in $1,250 every month is healthier than one that makes $3,000 in December and $200 in January. The score evaluates the predictability of revenue over time.

High score: Barbershop with monthly revenue between $1,200 and $1,350 over the past six months. Small variation, high consistency score.
Low score: Halloween costume store with 90% of revenue concentrated in October. High seasonality, low score on this pillar — which does not mean a bad business, but it demands careful cash flow planning.
How to improve: Create recurring revenue streams: subscriptions, monthly packages, maintenance plans. The more predictable the revenue, the healthier the business.

Taxes: MEI control with no surprises

Accountant Mode includes an automatic estimate of the DAS (MEI monthly flat tax payment) and monitors the annual revenue projection to alert you when you are approaching the legal limit.

MEI Limit 2024: R$ 81,000 per year = average of R$ 6,750 per month
How it works

Monthly DAS estimate

The system shows the fixed DAS amount for your MEI activity type (retail, service, or both) and projects annual revenue based on the average of months already logged.

Example 1 — Retail MEI: Fixed DAS of R$ 71.60/month (2024 table). The store billed R$ 4,200 in January and R$ 5,100 in February. Annual projection: R$ 55,800. Within the limit. No alert.
Example 2 — Growing revenue: Over the first 8 months, monthly average of R$ 7,500. Annual projection: R$ 90,000. Accountant Mode triggers the MEI limit alert before the tax authority acts — giving you time to plan the transition to the next business category.
Example 3 — Seasonal store: Party supply store with peaks in November and December. The system uses a weighted average and warns if the high months will push the annual total past R$ 81,000.
Note: Accountant Mode produces an estimate based on your logged entries. For official filings, always consult your accountant. The goal here is to give you visibility and predictability, not to replace professional guidance.

Best practices for accurate results

Accountant Mode is only as good as the data you feed it. Follow these practices and your reports will reflect the reality of the business — no distortions.

  • Log every transaction in the Daily Cash Register without exception — including exchanges, refunds, complimentary items and bank-transfer sales.
  • Use the correct categories: Sale for incoming revenue, Replenishment for goods purchased, Packaging/Shipping/Other for operating expenses.
  • Flag personal withdrawals as Personal — never log owner pay as a supplier expense.
  • Log purchases on the day payment leaves the register, not on the delivery date.
  • If you made an installment card sale, log the full amount on the day of the sale and record the card processing fee separately under Other.
  • Export the monthly P&L PDF and send it to your accountant — they will appreciate having organized data.
  • Review your Financial Score every month and note what changed: a drop in the Margin pillar is an immediate signal to revisit your pricing.
Final takeaway: Accountant Mode is not just a report — it is a thermometer. With an automatic P&L you know where every dollar goes, the Score shows the true health of the business, and the tax monitor means no nasty surprises from the tax authority. The retailer who knows their numbers makes better decisions, charges the right prices, and grows with confidence.
